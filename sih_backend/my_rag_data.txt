Diabetic retinopathy is the leading cause of visual loss in adults of the working-age group in the western population. It is the most common microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus. Diabetic retinopathy may lead to vision-threatening damage to the retina, eventually leading to blindness. Strict glycemic control, early detection, and appropriate management is the key to halting the progression of the disease. This activity reviews the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and management of diabetic retinopathy and highlights the role of the interprofessional team in evaluating and treating patients with this condition.

Diabetic retinopathy is a leading cause of preventable vision impairment and blindness in the WHO European

can be prevented by early detection and treatment through screening (Lee et al., 2015; Thomas et al., 2019;

health and social care resources and impact on economic productivity (WHO, 2019).

It can be prevented, and its progression slowed, by control of blood glucose, blood pressure and elevated lipids.
If it progresses to an advanced form, treatment with laser and, if available, intraocular drug injections can
prevent vision impairment and blindness.
Diabetic retinopathy screening can identify early changes in the retina so treatment can be given before vision
impairment or blindness occurs.

Diabetic retinopathy is a direct consequence of raised glucose levels on the small blood vessels of the retina.
Other important risk factors for diabetic retinopathy are raised blood pressure and elevated serum lipids, both of
which are common in people with diabetes.
People with diabetic retinopathy may not have any symptoms of vision impairment (asymptomatic).
When a person has the most advanced form of diabetic retinopathy, they can suddenly develop severe vision
impairment or blindness because of bleeding from abnormal retinal vessels into the eye or damage to the retina
from a retinal detachment.
As diabetic macular oedema increases in severity, the thickening of the retina affects the central part of the macula.
This damage can occur more slowly than in proliferative diabetic retinopathy, leading to progressive vision
impairment



Good blood glucose control and control of blood pressure and elevated lipids reduces the risk of new-onset
diabetic retinopathy and slows progression of existing diabetic retinopathy (Diabetes Control and Complications
Trial Research Group, 1993; Turner et al., 1998; Yau et al., 2012). This approach should underpin the prevention and
treatment of diabetic retinopathy in all people with diabetes. 
Laser treatment is the mainstay of treatment for proliferative diabetic retinopathy and can also be used for the
treatment of diabetic macular oedema (Wong et al., 2018).
Anti-VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) agents and steroids injected into the eye can reduce the progression
of the disease and preserve visual function in diabetic macular oedema (Wong et al., 2018). There are several
anti-VEGF agents, and bevacizumab is included in the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines (WHO, 2019b).
Vitrectomy can restore useful vision in eyes with non-resolving vitreous haemorrhage and traction retinal
detachment of the macula.


The first of the diabetic retinopathy stages is characterized by a balloon-like swelling in certain areas of the blood vessels in the retina called microaneurysms. This stage rarely affects vision or needs treatment, but it does signal diabetes damage has occurred and an increased risk of disease progression.

When minimal diabetic retinopathy is detected, providers can offer patient education on blood sugar management and reducing risks of complications from diabetes. In addition, providers should encourage patients to get diabetic retinopathy screenings every 6 months to monitor their eye health. 
The next diabetic retinopathy stage is characterized by damage to some of the blood vessels in the retina, resulting in leakage of blood and fluid into the retina tissue. This fluid can cause a loss of vision. 

When moderate diabetic retinopathy is detected, providers may refer patients to appropriate specialists, such as ophthalmology or endocrinology, as needed. Providers should work with patients to adjust their diet and blood sugar control regimens to slow the progress of the disease. Patients should continue to have retinal screenings to monitor eye health.
Severe NPDR

If there is continued inadequate control of diabetes, more blood vessels are damaged and blocked with even more leakage of blood and fluid into the retina, resulting in a much greater impact on vision. 

In cases of severe nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy, a timely referral to a retinal specialist is recommended. The patients should be monitored every 3 to 4 months and work with an endocrinologist or primary care provider on diabetes management. 
The most severe stage of Diabetic Retinopathy is Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy. There is extensive damage to the eye’s blood vessels and worsening circulation inside the eye. In response, the retina grows new blood vessels, but they tend to have abnormal formation and can cause severe damage.

At this point, the disease has advanced significantly and can result in vision loss or blindness very threatening to one’s vision. 

Patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy require immediate referral to a retina specialist for further examination and treatment. The patient may require injections of anti-VEGF drugs to stop the formation of new blood vessels. Laser treatments can be used to shrink abnormal vessels and reduce leaking. In some cases, a surgical vitrectomy to remove abnormal vessels and scarring may be appropriate.


Most people begin to develop diabetic retinopathy within 5 to 15 years of developing diabetes. Progression depends on individual health and how well blood sugar is managed. 
There may not be any vision changes in the early stages of diabetic retinopathy. As the condition progresses, patients may notice dark, floating spots or streaks across their field of vision, dark or empty patches, blurred vision, or loss of vision.
Diabetic retinopathy damages the blood vessels in the eye, causing them to swell and leak. It can also lead to scarring or abnormal blood vessel formation in the eye. Complications include diabetic macular edema (DME), retinal detachment, and neovascular glaucoma.

### Grade DR‑0 — No diabetic retinopathy
**Snippet:** DR‑0 — Normal retina; no microaneurysms, hemorrhages, exudates, venous changes, or neovascularization.

**Expanded text (use in generation):**
- **Clinical findings:** Fundus exam and retinal imaging show no microaneurysms, hemorrhages, hard exudates, venous beading, IRMA, or neovascularization.  
- **Symptoms:** None. Visual acuity typically normal.  
- **Risk factors for future DR:** Longer diabetes duration, poor glycemic control (high HbA1c), hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking, pregnancy.  
- **Recommended monitoring:** Dilated retinal exam annually (or per local guideline). Consider earlier follow‑up if systemic control worsens.  
- **Immediate management:** Maintain target glycemic control; optimize blood pressure and lipids; smoking cessation.  
- **Diet/lifestyle guidance:** Balanced meals with consistent carbohydrate distribution; emphasize whole grains, legumes, vegetables, lean protein, healthy fats; avoid sugar‑sweetened beverages; regular physical activity.

### Grade DR‑1 — Mild nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy (Mild NPDR)
**Snippet:** DR‑1 — Mild NPDR; microaneurysms only; usually asymptomatic; monitor for DME.

**Expanded text:**
- **Clinical findings:** Single or multiple retinal microaneurysms visible on ophthalmoscopy or fundus photography; no significant hemorrhages, venous beading, IRMA, or neovascularization.  
- **Symptoms:** Usually none; occasional mild blurring if macula affected.  
- **Risk/progression:** Low-to-moderate risk of progression; better systemic control reduces progression probability.  
- **Diagnostics:** Dilated fundus exam; fundus photography; OCT if macular involvement suspected.  
- **Ophthalmic management:** Routine surveillance (6–12 months depending on local protocol). Educate patient on symptom red flags (floaters, sudden vision loss).  
- **Systemic management:** Tighten glycemic, BP, and lipid targets as individualized by care team.  
- **Diet/lifestyle:** Carbohydrate consistency or counting; low‑GI choices; fiber and protein with meals; limit refined carbs and added sugars.

---

### Grade DR‑2 — Moderate nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy (Moderate NPDR)
**Snippet:** DR‑2 — Moderate NPDR; dot‑blot hemorrhages, hard exudates, early venous changes; monitor closely for DME.

**Expanded text:**
- **Clinical findings:** Increased microaneurysms and dot‑blot hemorrhages; hard exudates near macula possible; mild venous beading and early IRMA may appear; no neovascularization.  
- **Symptoms:** Often asymptomatic; may have intermittent blurring or metamorphopsia if macula involved.  
- **Risk/progression:** Elevated risk of progression to severe NPDR or PDR compared with DR‑1; presence of DME is main cause of vision loss.  
- **Diagnostics:** Dilated exam, wide‑field or standard fundus photography, OCT for macular edema, fluorescein angiography if ischemia/leakage assessment needed.  
- **Ophthalmic management:** Increase surveillance frequency (commonly every 3–6 months). Treat DME with intravitreal anti‑VEGF if center‑involving; focal laser for selected cases.  
- **Systemic management:** Intensify glycemic and BP control; statin therapy for dyslipidemia as indicated.  
- **Diet/lifestyle:** Structured meal plan; reduce simple sugars and refined carbs; Mediterranean‑style emphasis (vegetables, legumes, fish, olive oil); portion control and weight management.

---

### Grade DR‑3 — Severe nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy (Severe NPDR)
**Snippet:** DR‑3 — Severe NPDR; meets 4–2–1 rule (4 quadrants hemorrhages, 2 quadrants venous beading, or 1 quadrant IRMA); urgent retina referral.

**Expanded text:**
- **Clinical findings:** Any one of: severe hemorrhages in 4 quadrants; venous beading in ≥2 quadrants; IRMA in ≥1 quadrant. No frank neovascularization yet.  
- **Symptoms:** May be asymptomatic early; progressive blurring, increased floaters, or visual field defects may develop.  
- **Risk/progression:** Very high risk of progression to proliferative DR within 1 year if untreated.  
- **Diagnostics:** Urgent dilated retinal exam, OCT, and fluorescein angiography as indicated.  
- **Ophthalmic management:** Prompt referral to retina specialist; consider panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) to reduce progression risk; treat DME aggressively (anti‑VEGF). Close follow‑up (weeks to months).  
- **Systemic management:** Rapid optimization of glycemic control under supervision to avoid hypoglycemia; aggressive BP and lipid control; smoking cessation.  
- **Diet/lifestyle:** Medical nutrition therapy with dietitian; strict carbohydrate management; avoid large glycemic variability; coordinate nutrition with planned ophthalmic interventions.

---

### Grade DR‑4 — Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR)
**Snippet:** DR‑4 — Proliferative DR; neovascularization, vitreous/preretinal hemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment; urgent treatment required.

**Expanded text:**
- **Clinical findings:** Neovascularization on or near optic disc (NVD) or elsewhere (NVE); preretinal or vitreous hemorrhage; fibrovascular proliferation; possible tractional retinal detachment.  
- **Symptoms:** Floaters, sudden or progressive vision loss (often from vitreous hemorrhage), distorted vision; may lead to severe visual impairment.  
- **Risk/progression:** Vision‑threatening; without timely treatment, high risk of permanent vision loss.  
- **Diagnostics:** Urgent dilated exam, OCT, fluorescein angiography; B‑scan ultrasound if vitreous hemorrhage obscures view.  
- **Ophthalmic management:** Urgent retina specialist care. PRP laser and/or intravitreal anti‑VEGF injections to regress neovascularization; vitrectomy for non‑clearing vitreous hemorrhage or tractional RD. Post‑treatment monitoring for recurrence.  
- **Systemic management:** Intensive multidisciplinary diabetes care; manage anemia, renal disease, and other comorbidities that worsen retinal ischemia.  
- **Diet/lifestyle:** Maintain stable glycemia; coordinate perioperative nutrition and medication adjustments; ensure adequate protein and micronutrient intake for healing; avoid smoking and alcohol excess.

---

### Diabetic macular edema (DME) — cross‑grade summary
**Snippet:** DME — Fluid in macula from leaky vessels; can occur at any DR grade; main cause of vision loss.

**Expanded text:**
- **Definition:** Retinal thickening and/or hard exudates at the macula due to vascular leakage.  
- **Symptoms:** Blurred central vision, metamorphopsia, difficulty reading or recognizing faces.  
- **Diagnostics:** OCT is the gold standard for detecting and quantifying macular edema; fluorescein angiography helps localize leakage.  
- **Treatment:** First‑line for center‑involving DME is intravitreal anti‑VEGF therapy; focal/grid laser may be used for non‑center DME; corticosteroid implants considered in selected cases. Systemic risk factor control improves outcomes.  
- **Diet/lifestyle:** Same systemic targets as for DR; weight loss and BP control reduce DME risk.

### Practical patient‑facing diet and lifestyle checklist (concise)
- **Glycemic targets:** Individualized HbA1c goals set by diabetes team; avoid large postprandial spikes.  
- **Carbohydrate strategy:** Consistent carbohydrate intake; prefer low‑GI carbs; pair carbs with protein/fiber.  
- **Fats & lipids:** Favor monounsaturated/polyunsaturated fats; limit saturated/trans fats.  
- **Sodium:** Moderate intake to support BP control.  
- **Weight & activity:** Aim for gradual weight loss if overweight; 150 min/week moderate activity as tolerated.  
- **Alcohol & smoking:** Limit alcohol; stop smoking.  
- **Micronutrients:** No routine supplements for DR prevention; correct deficiencies as clinically indicated.  
- **When to seek urgent care:** New floaters, flashes, curtain/veil over vision, or sudden vision loss.
